# Denver Has 2,083 Active Short-Term Rental Licenses and 2,080 License Holders

Denver's short-term rental licenses are filed one per person under personal names, which is the shape the program's primary-residence rules require. Of the 2,083 licenses with an active status on October 19, 2026, 2,077 (99.7 percent) are the only active license held under their licensee name; three licensees hold two each, and no licensee holds three or more. Not one active license is filed under a name carrying a business-entity marker (an LLC, a corporation, a trust, a company). That count is a classification of the name as filed, not of beneficial ownership, and the register contains no field that could reveal common ownership or control behind those names. What clustering the register does show sits in two other places: on parcels, where 12 multi-license parcels hold 84 active licenses, each license under a different name; and in neighborhoods, where five neighborhoods hold 26.1 percent of the city's active licenses and the highest-density neighborhood runs at 27.1 licenses per 1,000 housing units, 4.7 times the citywide rate.

All figures below come from the short-term rental license register published by the Department of Licensing and Consumer Protection (DLCP) in the Denver Open Data Catalog (formerly Excise and Licenses; the dataset is still published under the Excise and Licenses name), pulled in full on October 19, 2026: 2,338 records. A license is counted as active in this brief only when its status reads exactly "License Issued - Active" (2,083 records). The same published table also carries 137 Delinquent, 116 Closed - Expired, 1 About to Expire, and 1 Closed - Surrendered record; those are counted separately wherever they appear and never merged into "active." This brief reports the licensed footprint only. Rentals operating without a license are not in this register and cannot be counted from it.

## Finding 1: By licensee name as filed, licenses sit one per person: 2,080 holders for 2,083 active licenses

Counted on the licensee name as filed (normalized for case and spacing; the counting rule is in Methods), the active register holds 2,083 licenses under 2,080 distinct names. The distribution, by licenses held per licensee: 1 license, 2,077 licensees; 2 licenses, 3 licensees; 3 to 5 licenses, 0 licensees; 6 to 10, 0; 11 or more, 0. The largest single holder accounts for 2 of 2,083 active licenses (0.10 percent). The top 5 holders together account for 8 licenses (0.38 percent), the top 10 for 13 (0.62 percent), and the top 25 for 28 (1.34 percent). Licensees holding two or more active licenses hold 6 licenses between them, 0.29 percent of the active register. Licensees holding five or more hold none.

The result is the same on the full register, every status included: 2,338 licenses under 2,333 names, five names with two licenses each, none with more. It also survives a harder test of the name field. Licensee identity here is a name string, and the obvious objection is that one operator could be split across name variants (a middle initial present on one filing, absent on another). Re-counting with names merged on first and last token only, ignoring middle names and initials, changes nothing: 2,080 groups, maximum two licenses, the same three pairs. An over-merge that groups by first initial and surname, which certainly combines different people who share common names, still produces a maximum group of four licenses and puts 92 licenses (4.4 percent) in groups of two or more; that is an upper bound on splitting by middle names and initials only, built by deliberately over-counting, not a measurement. It cannot bound splitting across a changed surname (marriage, hyphenation), a name-order swap, or a generational suffix keyed as the surname token. A row-level check of the three two-license holders (recorded in the package verification file) confirms each pair is two distinct license numbers, on two distinct parcels, at two distinct addresses: the three pairs are not duplicate filings of one license. Counted by address string instead of by holder, the register is just as flat: 2,082 distinct address strings for 2,083 active licenses, with exactly one address string carrying two licenses.

One pattern in the register sits in tension with the one-primary-residence rule, and it is printed here with no conclusion attached. The three two-license holders hold their pairs on distinct parcels at distinct addresses, and the pairs were originally issued years apart (2017 and 2026; 2022 and 2025; 2024 and 2026); two of the three pairs sit in different ZIP codes. Denver's rule contemplates one primary residence per person, so two simultaneous active licenses under one name is the most rule-relevant pattern in the register. Innocent mechanisms could produce it: a mid-term move with the old license not yet surrendered, or an overlapping renewal and new license in progress. The register cannot show which, if any, of these explains any pair, and no conclusion about any license follows from the count alone.

The name classification points the same way. Every active licensee name was classified by a documented token rule (Methods): a name is a business-entity name if any token is a business marker (LLC, INC, LTD, CORP, LLP, TRUST, COMPANY, and 53 other business tokens), an individual name if it is two to five alphabetic tokens with no business marker, and not determined otherwise. On all 2,083 active licenses, the result is: individual names 2,083 (100.0 percent), business-entity names 0 (0.0 percent), not determined 0 (0.0 percent). On all 2,080 active licensees the result is identical, and it is identical again on the full 2,338-record register. This is a classification of the name as filed, not of beneficial ownership: the register records a person's name for every license, and a person can of course operate a rental under their own name. What the field rules out, on its face, is the corporate-holder pattern: no active Denver STR license is held in the name of a company, trust, or partnership that the register can see.

Denver's STR license is, by the program's own rules, a primary-residence license. The Department of Licensing and Consumer Protection's short-term rental FAQ defines a primary residence as the place in which a person's habitation is fixed for the term of the license and is the person's usual place of return; a person can have only one primary residence, and the STR location is always a single residential dwelling unit (Sources). The eligibility consequence is direct: per Denver's STR rules the licensee is a person licensing their one primary residence, a single dwelling unit, so a name-field count cannot distinguish "no concentrated operators" from "intake only admits one license per natural person." The register would read flat under both worlds, including a world of concentrated property ownership behind the names, and it contains no field that could reveal common ownership behind them. This brief reports the pattern as measured; whether every licensed unit is in fact a primary residence is a question the register cannot answer, and nothing here tests it.

## Finding 2: Licenses stack on parcels, not on holders: 12 parcels hold 84 active licenses, each under a different name

The parcel join tells a different story from the holder count, and the two must not be confused. Bridged to the assessor parcel file (2,080 of 2,083 active licenses matched to a parcel, 99.9 percent; the bridge is described in Methods), the active licenses sit on 2,008 distinct parcels. On 1,996 of those parcels there is exactly one active license. On 12 parcels there are two or more: three parcels with two, four with three, one with four, two with six, one with 18, and one parcel in the Union Station neighborhood with 32 active licenses. Those 12 parcels hold 84 active licenses, 4.0 percent of the city's active total. On the full register the same shape reads 13 multi-license parcels, a maximum of 35 licenses on the largest, and 93 licenses on multi-license parcels in total.

The stacking is on parcels, not on holders. On each of the 12 multi-license parcels, every active license is held under a different licensee name: the 84 licenses belong to 84 different holders. The largest parcel's own assessor record carries 579 units; its 32 active licenses are 32 separate filings, not one holder with 32 licenses.

Parcels are not buildings, and the distinction matters here: condominium units are individual parcels with their own parcel numbers, so a parcel rollup splits condo buildings across parcels by construction. Grouping the same licenses by base address instead (the address with unit designators stripped systematically; the rule is in Methods) gives the building-level picture. The 2,083 active licenses sit at 1,963 base addresses. At 39 base addresses there are two or more active licenses, holding 159 licenses between them (7.6 percent of the active register); the largest count at one base address is 17. Of the 39 multi-license base addresses, 27 span more than one parcel (the condo pattern: one building, many unit parcels) and 12 sit on a single parcel. Within every multi-license base address, each license again sits under a different holder name.

One parcel record needs a sentence of its own. One of the 12 multi-license parcels carries 4 active licenses against a TOT_UNITS of 1 on its assessor record; the other multi-license parcels sit on records of 125 to 579 units, except one 2-license parcel whose record is a vacant common-element parcel with a TOT_UNITS of 0. Read closely, the TOT_UNITS of 1 record is not a single-unit house: it is classified RESIDENTIAL-CONDOMINIUM, its legal description identifies it as the master parcel of a condominium development (the record carries the master appendage, and a 126,465 square foot condo structure built in 2018), it carries no characteristics record in the spine, and all four licenses on it are unit filings by four different holders at addresses carrying unit designators, assigned to the master parcel by the bridge. The TOT_UNITS of 1 describes the master parcel record, not the building's unit count.

This is the pattern of multi-unit buildings licensed unit by unit. A reader who counted licenses per parcel and reported the result as licenses per operator would overstate holder concentration by exactly this mechanism; the counts are printed separately throughout this brief for that reason.

## Finding 3: The footprint covers 75 of 78 neighborhoods, and density runs from zero to 27.1 licenses per 1,000 housing units

Matched to parcels and through them to the city's 78 statistical neighborhoods, 2,080 of the 2,083 active licenses (99.9 percent) carry a neighborhood; the 3 unassigned licenses are the 3 the parcel bridge could not place. Active licenses appear in 75 of 78 neighborhoods. The three with none are Auraria, Kennedy, and Sun Valley. Those three are land-use zeros, not market zeros: Auraria is a campus, Sun Valley is largely public housing and redevelopment land, and Kennedy is a golf-course area. The DIA statistical neighborhood, at the other end of the map, carries 3 active licenses.

By count, the largest footprints are Five Points (151 active licenses), Highland (112), West Colfax (105), Berkeley (88), and Sunnyside (88). The five largest together hold 544 licenses, 26.1 percent of the city's active total; the ten largest hold 41.5 percent. Five Points alone holds 7.2 percent.

Counts favor big neighborhoods, so the exposure measure is licenses per 1,000 housing units (ACS 2020-2024 5-year housing units allocated to the statistical neighborhoods; basis in Methods). Citywide, Denver carries 5.8 active licenses per 1,000 housing units, which is 0.58 percent of the housing stock on the ACS count (359,351 allocated units). The median neighborhood rate is 4.7 per 1,000 (median across all 78 neighborhoods, zero-license neighborhoods counted as 0; over the 75 neighborhoods with licenses it would be 5.04). The highest rates are Whittier (27.1 per 1,000 units, 61 licenses), Cole (24.8, 50 licenses), Skyland (19.7, 35), Clayton (19.1, 35), Berkeley (18.4, 88), and Sunnyside (16.5, 88). Whittier's rate is 4.7 times the citywide rate: in Whittier, about 2.7 percent of housing units carry an active STR license. The high-density set is geographically coherent: the central and north-central neighborhoods (Whittier, Cole, Skyland, Clayton, Five Points) plus the northwest set (Berkeley, Sunnyside, Highland, West Colfax) account for most of the top of both tables, while the city's outer neighborhoods run at or below the median. These are licenses per housing unit, not per resident and not per rental unit; neighborhoods differ in tenure mix, and the register does not record which licensed units are rented how often. Because the denominator counts every housing unit, including owner-occupied homes, the ranking partly reflects each neighborhood's tenure mix as well as its licensed STR intensity; no per-renter-occupied-unit sensitivity was computed in this brief. The top of the rate table also rests on small counts over area-allocated denominators, so small gaps between adjacent rates (Whittier at 27.1 and Cole at 24.8, for example) sit within plausible allocation noise and should not be read as a precise ordering.

## Finding 4: The licensed footprint sits where recent construction sits (and this claim needs the second layer)

Joined against the warehouse permit layer on the parcel spine, the STR footprint and recent construction footprint overlap at the neighborhood level. Across the 75 neighborhoods compared (at least 1,000 housing units; DIA excluded by name as a single-use airport district), the rank correlation between a neighborhood's active STR licenses per 1,000 housing units and its new-building permits per 1,000 housing units (permits issued January 1, 2020 to October 12, 2026, surviving-parcel basis) is 0.64. Both rates divide by the same ACS housing allocation, so the shared-denominator objection applies and was tested: computed on raw counts instead of rates, the rank correlation is 0.62, and including DIA it is 0.60. Three neighborhoods appear in both top-10 lists (Berkeley, Sunnyside, West Colfax). The correlation is a geographic statement, not a causal one: it says the neighborhoods that added homes fastest in this decade are also the neighborhoods with the densest licensed STR presence, and it does not say either produced the other.

At the parcel level the same overlap appears in a different form. Of the 2,008 parcels holding an active STR license, 10.6 percent carry at least one new-building permit issued in the 2020 to 2026 window on the spine; among the 209,805 residential parcels with a characteristics record, the share is 3.6 percent. STR parcels are also more likely to carry a demolition in the same window (4.8 percent against 1.3 percent). The parcel comparison is cross-sectional: the spine describes parcels as they stand at the October 2026 warehouse snapshot, a permit on the parcel does not date the license, and newer or rebuilt homes may simply be more likely to be licensed. The claim this layer supports is co-location, stated above, and nothing stronger.

## Finding 5: The active register is mostly recent, and its own status and date fields disagree on 47 active records

The active register is mostly new. By original issue year, the 2,083 active licenses were first issued as follows: 31 in 2016, 73 in 2017, 65 in 2018, 129 in 2019, 88 in 2020, 179 in 2021, 247 in 2022, 258 in 2023, 247 in 2024, 401 in 2025, and 365 in 2026 to date. 61.0 percent of active licenses were originally issued in 2023 or later, and 36.8 percent in 2025 or 2026. Among licenses active today, the center of gravity is the current decade's second half, not the program's 2016 start. That distribution describes licenses active today, not the program's issuance history: lapsed licenses exit this table (the whole table holds only 255 non-active records across a decade of annual licensing, and 12 records left in the four days between the October 15 and October 19 snapshots), so the issue-year distribution is a survivor stock, not an issuance series.

The register also turns over measurably on a four-day scale. Between the October 15 and October 19, 2026 snapshots, total records fell from 2,349 to 2,338 (12 license numbers left the table, 1 entered) and active licenses fell from 2,098 to 2,083, a net change of minus 15 composed of 11 active licenses that left the register, 7 that moved from Active to Delinquent, 2 that moved from Delinquent back to Active, and 1 that entered as Active. No continuing license changed its licensee name or its parcel number. Delinquent records rose from 133 to 137 over the same four days. A point-in-time count of this register is a snapshot of a moving table, and this brief's figures are dated for that reason.

Two field-level disagreements are printed because a future reader of this register will meet them. First, 47 active-status licenses (2.3 percent of the active register) carry an expiration date earlier than the October 19, 2026 pull date, some by years. The cross-tab for those 47 points in two directions at once: the expiration lag is a median of 230 days and a maximum of 1,374 days, and 20 of the 47 expired more than a year before the pull; yet the MOST_RECENTLY_ISSUED_DATE on the same records falls in 2025 or 2026 for 29 of the 47 (by year: 2023, 2; 2024, 16; 2025, 20; 2026, 9), and 4 of the 47 carry a most-recent issue date after their printed expiration date. City guidance allows a complete pending renewal to continue operating while it is reviewed, and an expiration field that does not advance on renewal would produce the same shape; the register alone cannot say which field is authoritative, or whether either is wrong. Second, the table's one About to Expire record carries an expiration date in August 2026, already past at pull time. Both are disagreements between the register's own fields, recorded here as published; what they mean for any individual license's standing is a question for the licensing office, and this brief draws no conclusion from them about any license.

## Data notes: what this register is and is not

- The published table is named "active or pending renewal." Its contents are wider: as pulled, 10.9 percent of its records are Delinquent or Closed. Any count quoted from this table without a status filter overstates the active register by 255 records. Every figure in this brief names its status basis.
- The register measures licenses, not activity. It does not record nights rented, revenue, or whether a licensed unit is currently listed on any platform. A licensed unit may be inactive in the market, and an unlicensed rental leaves no row here at all.
- Licensee identity is the ENTITY_NAME field as filed. The concentration results are statements about that field, tested against name-variant merging in Finding 1. Household-level or manager-level common control exercised through different people's names cannot be seen in this field, and this brief does not estimate it. Per Denver's STR rules the licensee is a person licensing their one primary residence, a single dwelling unit, so a name-field count cannot distinguish "no concentrated operators" from "intake only admits one license per natural person"; the register has no field that could reveal common ownership behind the names.
- Property-ownership concentration was not tested in this brief. The warehouse's assessor records carry owner names, and no licenses-per-assessor-owner count was run, so nothing here speaks to how the licensed properties are owned, only to the names the licenses are filed under.
- Three active licenses (0.1 percent) could not be matched to a parcel by key or by address and are excluded from parcel and neighborhood tables only; they are counted in every citywide total.
- The register is republished continuously (the service's last edit before this pull was the same morning, October 19, 2026). Counts in this brief describe the October 19, 2026 snapshot, archived in the warehouse; a later pull will differ in detail, as the four-day drift in Finding 5 shows.

## What this might mean

This section is interpretation; the findings above are the measurements.

Read against Denver's primary-residence rule, the register's shape is what the rule predicts: licenses held one to a person, in personal names, spread across three quarters of the city's neighborhoods, densest in the central neighborhoods where housing churn and new construction are also densest. The strongest alternative explanation is that the register shows the compliant segment only: hosts who license are the hosts the rule fits, while rentals that do not fit the rule, if they operate, operate without licenses and are invisible here. A second alternative is that common control can be organized through people rather than entities (partners, family members, or a manager whose clients each file in their own name), in which case holder counts on a name field would read flat even where coordination exists. The data in this package cannot distinguish those worlds; they differ in what happens off-register. What the register does settle is narrower and still useful in a budget and policy debate: within the licensed system, by licensee name as filed, there is no holder concentration and no corporate-name holding, and no parcel or base address stacks licenses under a single licensee. That is a classification of the name as filed, not of beneficial ownership. Arguments about Denver STRs that assume a licensed market dominated by multi-unit operators are not describing this register.

## Who can act

- The Department of Licensing and Consumer Protection (DLCP), which administers the STR program and publishes this register (formerly Excise and Licenses; the dataset is still published under the Excise and Licenses name). Two low-cost operational items follow from Findings 1 and 5: publish a holder-count summary with the dataset (distinct licensees and the licenses-per-holder distribution are computable from the published fields, and publishing them would let any reader reproduce Finding 1 without a parcel join), and publish documentation of which field is authoritative when status and expiration disagree, so a reader of the table knows whether LICENSE_STATUS or EXPIRATION_DATE states a license's current term for the 47 records whose status reads Active while their expiration date has passed. Cost class: staff time inside the existing open-data program. How we would know it worked: the holder summary is published with the dataset and the field documentation exists, checkable by re-running this brief's counts against the published definitions.
- Denver City Council, during the 2027 budget deliberation now open (final budget proposal due October 19; public hearing October 26; amendment vote November 2; final passage November 9, the charter deadline). The decision this brief informs is what the licensing system can and cannot evidence: the register, as published, supports holder, parcel, and neighborhood accounting of the licensed market (Findings 1 to 4), and it cannot evidence the unlicensed market at all (Data notes). Any enforcement appropriation aimed at unlicensed rentals needs a data source this register does not contain; funding the register's upkeep and funding unlicensed-market measurement are different lines. Cost class: budget decision. How we would know it worked: a published, dated count of the unlicensed segment from whatever source the city adopts, comparable against this brief's licensed baseline of 2,083 active licenses.
- Neighborhood organizations in the high-density neighborhoods (Whittier, Cole, Skyland, Clayton, Berkeley, Sunnyside). The rates in Finding 3 are per housing unit; the organizations working in those neighborhoods hold the block-level knowledge (which buildings, which blocks) that a neighborhood rate cannot supply, and the parcel pattern in Finding 2 (multi-unit buildings licensed unit by unit) is most visible at that scale. Cost class: none. How we would know it worked: the same panel, republished with each register refresh, shows whether neighborhood rates move.

## Methods

- Dataset: ODC_short_term_rental_licenses_active_or_pending_renewal (Denver Open Data Catalog, ArcGIS FeatureServer layer 54), full-service pull October 19, 2026 (2,338 records), archived as the warehouse snapshot for that date with its pull log. The prior snapshot (October 15, 2026, 2,349 records) is the drift baseline and the source of the cleaned, parcel-bridged layer used for the join.
- Active basis: LICENSE_STATUS equal to "License Issued - Active" exactly. The full status counter was recorded before any filter was applied (Active 2,083; Delinquent 137; Closed - Expired 116; About to Expire 1; Closed - Surrendered 1) and sums to the 2,338 pulled records. LICENSE_TYPE is "Short Term Rental" on every record. One license record is the unit of count unless a figure is labeled holders, parcels, or neighborhoods.
- Holder identity: ENTITY_NAME normalized (uppercase, whitespace collapsed). Concentration is computed on the normalized string; two merge sensitivities are printed in Finding 1 (first-and-last-token merge; first-initial-and-surname over-merge). TAX_ID, present in the raw service, was not carried into any analysis file, following the warehouse layer's data-minimization rule.
- Name classification: tokens are the alphabetic words of the normalized name (hyphens and apostrophes treated as within-name characters). Business-entity name if any token is in the documented business token set (LLC, INC, LTD, CORP, CORPORATION, LLP, LLLP, LP, PC, PLLC, COMPANY, CO, TRUST, HOLDINGS, PROPERTIES, REALTY, VENTURES, PARTNERS, GROUP, CAPITAL, INVESTMENTS, ENTERPRISES, MANAGEMENT, ESTATES, HOMES, RENTALS, FOUNDATION, ASSOCIATION, BANK, CHURCH, UNIVERSITY, and related tokens listed in the build code). Individual name if the name has two to five alphabetic tokens and no business token. Not determined otherwise. Shares are computed on all active licenses (and separately on all active holders), with determined and not-determined shares summing to 100 percent.
- Parcel bridge: the fresh snapshot inherits the warehouse Phase 3 bridge by license number (key 2,321; address-strict 13; unmatched 3); the one license that entered after October 15 was key-matched on its parcel number against the parcel spine (1 more key match), for a fresh match rate of 99.87 percent. Neighborhood is the parcel spine v6 neighborhood of the assigned parcel (spine built October 15, 2026). The source field MULTIPLE_LICENSES reads "Stand Alone" on all 2,338 records and was not used; multi-license parcels are computed from the bridge.
- Base-address rollup (Finding 2): addresses normalized (uppercase, punctuation removed, whitespace collapsed), then unit designators stripped systematically: a trailing named designator (UNIT, APT, APARTMENT, STE, SUITE, BLDG, BUILDING, FL, FLOOR, RM, ROOM) or # symbol plus its identifier, or a bare trailing unit token (digits, digits plus a letter, or a single letter) following a street-suffix token. Licenses are then grouped by the resulting base address.
- Rates: active licenses per 1,000 housing units. Denominators are ACS 2020-2024 5-year estimates (table B25001) at tract level for Denver County (359,581 units), allocated to statistical neighborhoods in proportion to tract area inside each neighborhood polygon (359,351 units allocated), the same denominators as the October 17 and 18 briefs. Rates compare neighborhoods within the snapshot; no rate is printed for a geography smaller than a statistical neighborhood. The median neighborhood rate is computed across all 78 neighborhoods, zero-license neighborhoods counted as 0.
- Layering admission test, applied per the brief-writing standards: the ACS housing layer is used because it converts raw neighborhood counts into exposure rates (Finding 3 could not rank neighborhoods without it); the parcel spine is used because it assigns each license a parcel and a neighborhood and exposes the parcel-stacking pattern (Finding 2 cannot be stated from the register alone); the permit layer is used because it supports the co-location claim in Finding 4, which no single source contains. One tested layer was cut: demolition counts by neighborhood were computed and are reported only as the parcel-level prevalence inside Finding 4, because the neighborhood-level demolition comparison added no claim beyond the permit comparison.
- Verification: a pre-flight audit script (Section 10 gates) ships in this package's code folder and its output is recorded in the verification file, including two built-in controls: recomputed licenses-per-parcel from the raw October 15 snapshot reproduces the cleaned layer's per-parcel counts on all 2,265 parcels with zero mismatches, and the three two-license holders were checked row by row (distinct license numbers, parcels, and addresses). Every figure in this brief is cross-checked against the package stats file.
